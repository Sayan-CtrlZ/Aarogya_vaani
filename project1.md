AarogyaVaani A voice-first, offline patient intake tool for primary health centres Hack for Social Cause | VBYLD 2027 | Theme: Healthcare, Wellbeing & Service Delivery | State focus: Tripura 1. Problem Statement State: Tripura At primary health centres in Tripura, a doctor often has only a few minutes with each patient. Many patients speak Bengali or Kokborok, and many rural women cannot read. A full history is rarely taken. In pregnancy, danger signs such as bleeding, severe headache or fits can be missed. AarogyaVaani is an offline, voice-led intake tool. It gathers a complete history before the consultation and warns the health team about danger signs. Target beneficiaries • Pregnant women and other patients at public primary health facilities, especially in rural and tribal areas. • ASHA workers and ANMs, who do the first contact and most of the paperwork. • Doctors at PHCs and CHCs, and hub doctors who consult by tele-consultation. Current gaps • Very short consultations. India's average primary care consultation is about 2 minutes [1]. • Language and literacy. Bengali is the mother tongue of 63.43% of people in Tripura and Kokborok of 23.97% [3][4]. Nearly 1 in 4 rural women aged 15 to 49 are not literate [2]. • Too few doctors. About a third of medical officer and specialist posts are vacant [2]. • History is on paper. Records are scattered, so each visit starts from zero, and a doctor joining by tele-consultation has no history to read [8]. • Existing tools do not fit. Registration systems capture only name and age. Health apps need a smartphone, internet and prior sign-up [8]. 2. The first step of history-taking should move to the patient and the health worker, with the app as the guide. • About nine in ten institutional deliveries happen in public facilities. A tool placed in public facilities reaches almost every woman who delivers in a facility. • Language and literacy rule out typing and reading. Voice and pictures are needed. Kokborok is written in both Bengali and Roman script [5], which is one more reason to rely on voice and icons. 3. The Solution AarogyaVaani runs on an Android phone or tablet kept at the health centre. The patient, or an ASHA or ANM on the patient's behalf, answers a guided interview by speaking or by tapping pictures. The app asks follow-up questions the way a doctor would, watches for danger signs, and builds a short summary for the doctor. The summary is read back to the patient in their own language. The app works without internet and sends data to the health record when the network returns. Nothing is recorded without the patient's agreement. The app never makes a diagnosis. The doctor reviews, edits and approves every summary. Who it helps • The patient: is understood in their own language, with no reading or typing. • The health worker (ASHA or ANM): is guided step by step, with less paperwork. • The doctor, on site or remote: reads a complete history in seconds. How a visit works 1. Choose language and give consent. A short audio message explains what the app will do. The patient agrees by voice or by tapping. 2. Answer the interview by voice or touch. An ASHA or ANM can run it in assist mode. 3. The app asks follow-up questions and checks each answer for danger signs. An urgent sign sends an instant alert to the nurse. 4. The app builds the summary and reads it back to the patient. 5. The doctor reads, edits and approves. Saved data syncs when the network is available. Languages The first release supports Bengali and Hindi, with picture screens for every question. Kokborok voice is planned for a later release. We found public Kokborok speech datasets and early community experiments, but no ready voice service [10]. Until then, Kokborok-speaking patients use the picture screens with help from a health worker who speaks the language. Where it fits AarogyaVaani sits in front of eSanjeevani, the national telemedicine service. In eSanjeevani, a health worker at the local centre does a basic consultation first and then connects the patient to a doctor at a hub [6]. Our summary gives that hub doctor the history before the call starts. Safeguards • The doctor approves every summary. The app gives no diagnosis. • Danger-sign checks use fixed medical rules built from standard lists of pregnancy danger signs [7][9]. A doctor or senior nurse should review the final list. • The app reads back what it heard so the patient can correct mistakes. • Consent follows India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. The patient can refuse or withdraw at any time. Features MVP means the feature is in the working demo. Roadmap means it comes later. No. Feature What it does Phase 1 Language and consent The patient picks a language. A short audio message explains what the app will do with their answers. They agree by voice or by tapping, and can refuse or withdraw later. Example: A patient hears the explanation in Bengali and taps "I agree". MVP 2 Voice-first intake Every question is spoken aloud and every answer can be spoken. Nothing needs to be read or typed. Example: "How many months pregnant are you?" The patient answers aloud and the app records it. MVP (Bengali, Hindi) 3 Touch and icon answers Any question can be answered by tapping big pictures: faces for yes and no, a body outline to show where it hurts, faces for pain levels. Example: The patient taps the stomach on a body picture. MVP 4 Adaptive follow-up questions The next question depends on the last answer, like a doctor's questions. When a problem is mentioned, the app asks when it started, what it feels like and what makes it better or worse. Example: "Headache" leads to "Since when?" and "Is your vision blurred?" MVP 5 Danger-sign alerts Fixed medical rules check every answer. If a serious sign appears, the patient is marked urgent and the nurse gets an instant alert. Example: Bleeding in pregnancy shows a red alert. MVP 6 Maternal and antenatal module A question set for pregnancy: months of pregnancy, earlier pregnancies, check-ups done, iron tablets, and the main danger signs (heavy bleeding, severe headache or blurred vision, swelling of face or hands, fits, less baby movement, high fever, severe stomach pain, repeated vomiting). Example: "Is the baby moving as much as usual?" A "no" is flagged at once. MVP (flagship) 7 Health worker assist mode An ASHA or ANM runs the same screens for patients who cannot use the device, such as the very old, the very unwell or first-time phone users. Example: An ASHA sits beside an elderly patient and taps the answers. MVP 8 Doctor summary A short, structured summary: main complaint, story of the problem, past illness, medicines, allergies and danger signs. The doctor edits, approves or rejects it. The same summary is read back to the patient. Example: The doctor reads a half-page summary instead of asking for four minutes. MVP 9 Offline use and auto-sync Everything runs on the device. Summaries are stored safely and sent to the health record when the network returns. Example: A hill-area centre with no signal still completes the full intake. MVP offline, sync on roadmap 10 Data protection and cleanup Temporary session data, such as audio and answers in progress, is deleted once the summary is saved. Summaries waiting to sync stay protected on the device. Example: After the doctor approves, the raw audio is gone. MVP 11 Document capture Photograph old prescriptions, lab reports or the mother and child card. The app reads them, picks out medicines and test results, and puts them in date order. Example: Three crumpled prescriptions become one dated list. Roadmap 12 Health record and tele-consult link Sends the approved summary to the patient's health record (ABHA, under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission) and to the hub doctor in a tele-consultation. Example: The hub doctor opens the summary before the video call. Impact and Scalability How we will measure impact We do not claim results yet. We will set numeric targets after a small pilot at one primary health centre. These are the measures. Measure What it shows Time for a first-time patient to finish the intake Whether it is usable without training Share of patients who finish without help Whether it works for low-literacy users Share of summary fields filled How complete the history is Danger signs flagged, compared with a nurse checklist Safety: missed signs and false alarms Time from urgent flag to nurse attention Speed of response Time the doctor takes to read the summary Time saved, against a consultation of about 2 minutes Doctor edits per summary Accuracy of the summary Scalability • One design fits every level of care. Tripura has 107 rural PHCs, 22 CHCs and 965 sub-centres [2], and the same intake runs at each. • It uses the health workforce already in place: 7,077 ASHAs [2]. No new staff are needed. • New modules (child health, chronic disease follow-up) and new languages are added as question sets and language packs.• The model can be adapted for other states by changing the language and question sets. • The code will be open source, as the hackathon requires. SDG alignment: SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities). 6. MVP and Roadmap What we build first, so it can be shown in a live demo and a 3 to 5 minute video. MVP • One use case: maternal and antenatal intake, with about 10 to 15 core questions. • Bengali and Hindi, with picture screens for every question. • Voice and touch answers, plus assist mode for the health worker. • Danger-sign rules for a small, well-chosen set of red flags, with an instant nurse alert. • A structured summary on a simple doctor's screen, with edit and approve. • Fully offline local storage and an audio consent screen. Roadmap • Kokborok voice support. • Document capture and reading. • Auto-sync, health record (ABHA) link and tele-consult hand-off. • General adult intake and chronic disease follow-up. 7.
